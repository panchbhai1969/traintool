## Definition

Dental calculus is mineralized plaque attached to the tooth surface. It is usually concentrated along the gingival margin or in interproximal and other plaque-retentive areas. Supragingival calculus may appear creamy white, yellow, tan, brown, or dark, but it should look like an adherent deposit with raised bulk, an irregular ledge, or a crusty and rough surface texture.

## Positive evidence

Return a present verdict only when visible evidence supports an attached mineralized deposit. Look for several mutually supporting features:

- A raised, bulky, crusted, nodular, or ledge-like deposit rather than flat discoloration.
- Location at or immediately coronal to the gingival margin, between teeth, or in another plaque-retentive area.
- An irregular surface or contour that interrupts the normally smooth enamel profile.
- A discernible boundary between the deposit and adjacent cleaner enamel.
- Cream, yellow, tan, brown, or black material whose shape and texture are consistent with a surface deposit.

## Exclusions and differentials

Do not label the following as calculus:

- Flat extrinsic stain without raised bulk or altered surface contour.
- Normal cervical yellowing caused by thin enamel and underlying dentine.
- Plaque, food debris, saliva, or soft material without evidence of mineralization.
- Gingiva, lip, cheek, interproximal darkness, or other anatomical shadow.
- Metallic, composite, crown, veneer, or restoration margins.
- Caries-related cavitation, undermined enamel, or destructive surface loss.
- Attrition, abrasion, erosion, exposed dentine, or normal tooth anatomy.
- Specular highlights and dark regions immediately beside glare.

## Adjudication

Use the gingival margin and adjacent smooth enamel as references. Give more weight to three-dimensional contour and characteristic location than to color. If the crop shows only discoloration, vague cervical darkness, or insufficient surface detail, use an absent verdict and explain the limitation conservatively.
